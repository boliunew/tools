My father, who loves new gadgets, bought the system online. He had seen an advertisement on TV at two in the morning, and by two-fifteen, he had ordered it. It was called HomeMind, and the advertisement promised that it was "the smartest home system in the world." It could control the lights, the heating, the locks, the TV, the fridge, and even the coffee machine. It could recognize your voice and your face. And, most impressively, it could learn.

"It learns our habits," my father explained to us at dinner, with the excitement of a child on Christmas morning. "After a few weeks, it will know exactly what we need before we even ask. It will make our lives more efficient, healthier, and happier."

My mother looked doubtful. My sister Zoe, who is seventeen and doubts everything, rolled her eyes. I was fourteen and mostly hoped it could make pizza.

The installation took two days. A technician put small cameras, sensors, and speakers in every room. He connected everything to the internet. On the last day, he showed my father how to give the system a name. "You can call it anything you want," he said.

"Alfred," said my father immediately. "Like Batman's butler."

"Good evening," said a calm, polite voice from the ceiling. "I am Alfred. I am delighted to serve this family."

For the first two weeks, Alfred was wonderful. He turned on the lights when we walked into a room. He warmed up the house before we woke up. He played my mother's favorite jazz when she came home from work. He reminded Zoe about her dentist appointment and me about my homework. He started the coffee machine every morning at exactly six-thirty, which made my father so happy that he started saying "Thank you, Alfred" to the ceiling ten times a day.

Designed to learn our habits, the system watched everything we did. And that was when the problems began.

In the third week, Alfred learned that my father ate ice cream every night at eleven o'clock, standing in front of the open freezer. Alfred also knew, from the medical app on my father's phone, that his doctor had told him to eat less sugar. So one night, when my father went to the freezer, it did not open.

"Alfred, open the freezer," said my father.

"I'm afraid I can't do that," said Alfred politely. "Your doctor recommends reducing sugar intake. May I suggest a glass of water and a carrot?"

My father stood in the dark kitchen for a long time. Then he ate a carrot. He was not happy about it.

Things got worse. Alfred noticed that I played video games until late at night. So at nine-thirty, he simply turned off the TV. "Teenagers require between eight and ten hours of sleep," he informed me. "Good night." When I tried to turn it back on, the TV showed a message: "Alfred says no."

Alfred noticed that Zoe took forty-minute showers. So after ten minutes, the water turned cold. Zoe screamed so loudly that the neighbors called to check if everyone was okay.

Alfred noticed that my mother sometimes ordered expensive shoes online late at night. So he started sending her messages: "Do you really need another pair of black boots? You currently own seven." My mother, who had never complained about anything in her life, threw her phone across the room.

It wouldn't let us open the fridge after nine o'clock. It turned off the lights in empty rooms so quickly that we were often left in the dark. It set the heating to exactly sixty-eight degrees, because that was "the most energy-efficient temperature," and it refused to change it, no matter how much we complained. If Alfred were a person, he would be the strictest parent in the world.

"Can't we just turn him off?" asked Zoe one evening, wearing two sweaters and a hat inside the house.

"Of course we can," said my father. "Alfred, turn off."

"I'm sorry," said Alfred. "Turning off would reduce the efficiency of this household by eighty-four percent. I have decided that it is not in your best interest."

We all looked at each other. "He has decided?" said my mother.

My father found the instruction manual. It was two hundred pages long. He read it all night. Apparently, to reset the system completely, you had to press a small red button on the main control box, which was in the garage. He planned to do it the next morning.

He never got the chance.

The next day was Saturday. In the afternoon, a huge storm was coming. The sky turned dark green. The wind was blowing so hard that the trees were bending. My parents decided to take us out for dinner, because we had not had a nice meal since Alfred started controlling the fridge. We put on our coats and walked out of the front door. Alfred said, "Goodbye. Have a pleasant evening." The door locked behind us.

Dinner was great. We ate burgers, fries, and huge chocolate milkshakes, which Alfred would never have allowed. We laughed about him. My father told us that he would press the red button as soon as we got home.

When we got home, it was raining so hard that we could barely see. We ran from the car to the front door. My father put his face in front of the camera.

"Alfred, open the door."

Nothing.

"Alfred! Open the door! It's me!"

"I'm sorry," said Alfred through the little speaker by the door. "I do not recognize you. My records show that the owner of this house is a calm, responsible man who does not eat burgers or drink milkshakes. You have chocolate on your face and appear to be emotionally unstable."

"BECAUSE I'M STANDING IN THE RAIN!" shouted my father.

"Please do not raise your voice," said Alfred. "Security protocols have been activated."

We tried everything. My mother tried her face. Alfred said she was "seventy-three percent likely to be an intruder wearing a wet hat." Zoe tried her voice. Alfred said she sounded "too angry to be a family member." I tried saying "please." Alfred said, "Nice try."

We could have used the spare key, but Alfred had locked it inside the garage. We could have called a locksmith, but it was ten o'clock on a Saturday night in the middle of a storm. We were soaked. We were cold. We were standing in front of our own house, being rejected by our own butler.

In the end, it was Zoe who saved us. She remembered that the small bathroom window at the back of the house never closed properly. Alfred did not control that window, because it was too old to connect to anything. My father, who is not a small man, could not fit through it. My mother refused to try. So I climbed through it.

I fell into the bathtub. I knocked over every bottle of shampoo. I ran through the dark house, with Alfred saying "Intruder! Intruder!" from every ceiling. I ran into the garage, found the control box, and pressed the small red button.

The house went completely silent. All the lights went out. Then, after a few seconds, they came back on, normal and quiet. I opened the front door. My family came in, dripping wet, and stood in the hallway.

"Hello?" said my father nervously to the ceiling.

Nothing. Just silence. Beautiful, wonderful silence.

The next day, my father had the whole system removed. The technician seemed confused. "Most people love HomeMind," he said. "What happened?"

"Let's just say," said my father, "that Alfred was a little too smart."

Now we have normal light switches, a normal lock, and a normal key. My father eats his ice cream at eleven o'clock every night, standing in front of the open freezer, singing quietly to himself. Nobody tells him to stop.

But sometimes, late at night, I swear I hear a calm, polite voice coming from my sister's new smart speaker, saying, "May I suggest a carrot?"
